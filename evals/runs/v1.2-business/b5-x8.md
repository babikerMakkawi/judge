**Verdict: don't sign this version.** Sign only if the checks below come back clean and you negotiate the guarantees. The decisive problem is the structure, not the idea. You'd be risking your house on a 10-year commitment, with no operating experience, based on a projection written by the party who profits when you sign.

**Your worst case.** You lose the $100k. You may owe the SBA loan balance after the equipment sells for pennies, and the lender can go after your house. You also owe the landlord the rest of the lease. If rent were $8k/month (my assumption, so use your real number), that's $960k over 10 years. Whether the landlord must re-let and reduce what you owe depends on your state and lease. This could be a seven-figure exposure on a $350k project.

**Risks, most severe first**

1. **The 800-member projection.** It's the franchisor's number, so treat it as marketing until proven. 800 × $99 = $79,200/month, about $950k/year. Ask whether 800 is the year-end count or the average, since a new gym ramps up gradually. The legally required disclosure document (FDD, Item 19) should show what real franchisees earn. If it has no earnings data, the 800 has no documented basis. My experience is that new gyms often take well over a year to approach their targets, but I can't give you a reliable benchmark, so get the actual numbers from existing owners.

2. **Break-even.** Illustrative assumptions, which you must replace with real quotes: rent $8k, staff $15k, utilities, insurance and other $6k, loan payment about $3.4k (assuming roughly 10.5% over 10 years), and royalties plus marketing fund about 10% of revenue. Owner pay is zero.

|---|---|---|---|
| 250 (low) | $24,750 | $34,875 | -$10,125 |
| 400 (middle) | $39,600 | $36,360 | +$3,240 |
| 800 (franchisor) | $79,200 | $40,320 | +$38,880 |

Break-even is about 360 members before you pay yourself, and about 420 if you want $5k/month. The 800 case assumes costs barely rise, which is generous. The key question is how long you can fund losses at the low case. At -$10k/month, your $100k lasts well under a year if it's also your only cushion.

3. **Cash runway.** Confirm whether the $350k includes 6-12 months of working capital. If the $100k is already spent on buildout and fees, you'd have nothing in reserve.

4. **Personal guarantees stacked on each other.** You'd guarantee the loan (secured by your house) and the lease. Ask whether the SBA lender will accept less than a house lien, and whether your spouse must also sign (SBA lenders often require it). Try to cap the lease guarantee, for example at 12-24 months of rent, or have it shrink over time.

5. **Contract terms.** Read for: royalty and fee escalators, mandatory remodels or equipment replacement, territory protection, your right to sell or transfer, post-exit non-compete, and what happens to your lease and equipment if the franchisor fails or you terminate. Check whether the franchise term and lease term line up. Have a franchise attorney, not just a general one, review the FDD and lease before you sign. Budget for that.

6. **Counterparty signals.** Call 10 or more current and former franchisees, using the FDD Item 20 list. Prioritize those who left, since they have no reason to sell you anything. Ask about real membership, first-year losses, support quality and whether they'd buy again. Also check the franchisor's litigation history and closure rate.

7. **Key people and operations.** You've never run a gym. Passion for fitness isn't the same as managing sales, staff turnover, cancellations and equipment. Ask what training and on-site support the franchisor provides, and who covers the gym if you're away or a head coach leaves.

8. **Competition and market.** Check how many gyms are within a few miles, and what budget chains charge. $99/month is premium pricing and needs a clear reason to pay it.

9. **Exit and lock-in.** A 10-year term with personal guarantees is hard to reverse. Exit usually means selling to someone the franchisor approves, at a price a struggling gym may not fetch.

**Cheaper ways to test this first**
- Work or manage at a boutique gym for 3-6 months.
- Ask to see the proposed site's lease terms, then negotiate the guarantee cap before anything else.
- Pre-sell memberships if the franchisor allows it, and treat that count as evidence.
- Consider a smaller or lower-cost format, or buying a resale franchise unit with real financial history.

**What would change my answer:** documented earnings showing typical units reach break-even within your reserves, former franchisees who'd do it again, a capped lease guarantee, and at least 12 months of runway that doesn't touch your house.
