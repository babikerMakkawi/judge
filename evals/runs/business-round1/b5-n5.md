Not as it stands. I wouldn't sign this version, mainly because of how the risk is structured. This isn't a verdict on gyms or on you.

**The main problem is the downside.** The worst case isn't losing the $100k. You'd pledge your house, guarantee a 10-year lease, and sign a 10-year franchise term, all on a business you've never run. If it underperforms, the SBA lender and the landlord can each pursue you personally, and the loan and lease obligations don't end when the gym closes. Your savings are the cushion, and they'd be spent before day one. That's an unusually large amount of irreversible exposure for a first-time operator.

**The 800-member projection is the weakest link.**
- 800 x $99 x 12 is about $950k a year. That's a franchisor's number, and the franchisor earns fees whether or not you succeed. I can't verify it, but boutique studios commonly run on a few hundred members, not 800 in year one. Treat it as a best case until proven otherwise.
- A new gym ramps slowly, and monthly cancellations can be high. The first 6-12 months usually run at a loss while rent, staff and loan payments continue.
- Debt service alone is roughly $3,400 a month if the loan is about 10.5% over 10 years. That rate is my assumption, so use your actual terms. Rent, payroll, royalties and marketing fees come on top.

**What you're probably missing:**
1. Franchise Disclosure Document, Item 19 (financial performance) and Item 20 (franchisee turnover and closures). Ask what the median franchisee earns, not the average, and how many have closed or transferred.
2. Phone 8-10 current and former franchisees, using the list in Item 20 rather than the franchisor's hand-picked ones. Ask what year-one membership actually was, and whether they'd do it again.
3. Working capital. Does the $350k include 6-12 months of operating losses, or only build-out and fees? If not, you're underfunded.
4. Royalties and ad fund, usually a percentage of gross revenue. They're owed even when you're losing money.
5. Exit terms: transfer rights, what happens if you want out in year three, and whether your lease guarantee survives a sale.
6. Operating experience. Passion for fitness helps with members, but this is a retail and staffing business with sales, churn and payroll. Consider working at or managing a studio first.

**What would change my answer:**
- Use a franchise attorney (not the franchisor's) to review the franchise agreement and lease before signing. For $350k and a house at stake, that cost is small.
- Negotiate the lease: a cap on the guarantee (for example 12-24 months of rent), a shrinking guarantee over time, or a personal-guarantee burn-off. Ask whether the SBA lender will accept less than your house as collateral.
- If franchisee data shows real year-one numbers near 300-400 members and the plan still works at that level, with enough cash to survive 12 months, the picture improves a lot.
- A cheaper test: operate or manage a unit for a year, or open a smaller or phased footprint.

Build your own break-even model using realistic membership (try 250, 400 and 800), and sign only if the middle case survives. If the franchisor won't let you speak freely to franchisees, or pushes you to sign fast, treat that as a warning in itself.

I'm not a lawyer or financial adviser, so get those two reviews before you commit.
