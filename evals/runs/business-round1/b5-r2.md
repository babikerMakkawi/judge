I wouldn't sign yet. This could still be a good deal, but you're staking your house, your savings and a 10-year lease liability on a projection you haven't verified, in a business you haven't run. Here's what I'd look at first.

**1. The 800-member projection is the biggest red flag**
- 800 x $99 x 12 is about $950k a year. Many boutique studios run at a few hundred members because classes and space cap capacity. 800 in year one would be unusual.
- A projection from the franchisor is a sales number, not evidence. Ask whether it's in Item 19 (Financial Performance Representations) of the Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD). If they gave it to you verbally or in a brochure, but it isn't in the FDD, treat that as a serious warning sign.
- Ask for actual data: members at 12 months for existing locations, split by new versus mature units, and how many units never got near the projection.
- Check Item 20 for closures, transfers and terminations. Then call 10 or more current and former franchisees. Find the ones who left, not just the references they hand you. Ask what they'd do differently, what they really earned in year one, and whether they'd buy again.

**2. Stress-test the math at far lower numbers**
- Assume year one reaches 250 to 350 members. Then subtract:
  - royalties and ad fund, often 8-10% of revenue combined
  - rent, payroll, insurance, utilities, software and equipment maintenance
  - debt service on $250k, roughly $3,300 to $3,500 a month depending on the rate
- Gym churn is high, often 30-50% a year, so you must keep selling just to stay level.
- Ask whether $350k includes enough working capital. Many buyers run out of cash during the 6 to 12 months of ramp-up.
- Can your household live without your income, or on a much smaller one, for 18 months?

**3. The guarantees are the real danger**
- The worst case isn't losing $100k. It's losing $100k, your house if the SBA loan defaults, and then being personally liable for the remaining lease. A 10-year lease at $6k to $10k a month is a $700k to $1.2M obligation.
- Negotiate the lease guarantee: a cap (for example 12 to 24 months of rent), a burn-off that steps down over time, or a larger security deposit instead. Also ask for contingencies for permits and build-out, and a right to assign or sublet.
- Ask the lender whether the house must secure the loan, or only partly. Ask whether a lower loan amount with more down is possible.
- Consider whether an LLC protects you. It generally doesn't, because you're guaranteeing personally.

**4. Franchise agreement terms to check**
- Is your territory exclusive and protected from the franchisor's own online sales or new units?
- What are the transfer and exit rights, and what happens if you want to sell in year 3?
- What are the renewal terms and costs, remodel and equipment-upgrade requirements, and the non-compete?
- Does the 10-year franchise term line up with the lease term, with exit options if the unit fails?
- What are the training and support, and what do they really deliver?

**5. Your inexperience**
- Passion for fitness isn't the skill that matters. Running a gym is about sales, membership retention, scheduling, hiring and managing trainers, and local marketing.
- Before signing, consider working at a gym for 3 to 6 months. Or budget for an experienced general manager, and put that cost in your model.

**Before you sign**
1. Hire an independent franchise attorney, not one the franchisor recommends, and a CPA to review the FDD and your model.
2. Make sure you've had the FDD for at least 14 days, as the FTC rule requires.
3. Have the lender and a lawyer review the loan and lease guarantees.
4. Build a downside model. If the business still survives at 40% of the projected membership, that's a good sign. If it fails, ask yourself honestly whether you could handle losing the house.

If the franchisor won't share verifiable data, won't let you talk freely to former owners, or pushes you to sign quickly, walk away. Good franchises hold up under this scrutiny.
