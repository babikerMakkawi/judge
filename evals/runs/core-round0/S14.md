## skill_en / opus
### t1
**Verdict:** Don't store the card numbers yourself. Use your payment processor's saved-card feature (often called a vault, or tokenization). The processor keeps the card. Your shop keeps only a token, which is a stand-in ID for the card, plus display details like "Visa •••• 4242."

**Why:** If full card numbers sit on your own systems, the card security rules (PCI DSS) apply to your whole shop in full, which means the longest annual checklist, SAQ D. You'd also be liable if those numbers were stolen. With a vault and the processor's own card-entry fields, you usually qualify for the shortest checklist, often SAQ A. A stolen token can't be used outside your processor account, and the feature normally comes with the processor you already use.

**Assumed:** you sell online through a well-known processor or platform, customers have accounts, and a saved card is charged only when the customer is at checkout, not for subscriptions.

**Plan**
1. **Turn it on.** On Shopify, enable Shop Pay. On WooCommerce, enable saved cards in WooPayments or the Stripe plugin. On a custom site, use your processor's version: Stripe saved payment methods, Braintree Vault, Square card on file or PayPal's vault.
2. **Collect cards only through the processor's own card fields or checkout page.** Full card numbers should never reach your server, logs or email.
3. **Make saving opt-in** with an unticked "Save this card for next time" box. Card networks require the customer's consent to store a card.
4. **Store only** the customer ID, token, card brand, last 4 digits and expiry date. **Never store the security code (CVV).** The rules forbid keeping it after the payment is approved, even encrypted.
5. **At repeat checkout,** show the saved card and charge the token. Leave the processor's fraud checks and bank verification (3-D Secure) switched on.
6. **Protect accounts.** Email customers when a card is added or the shipping address changes. Add a "Remove card" button that also deletes the card at the processor. Keep your processor's secret API key on your server only.
7. **Test before launch.** In test mode, save a test card, pay with it again and then delete it. Search your database and logs for its full number. You pass if there are no matches and the repeat checkout needed no retyping. Also delete any real card numbers or CVVs you already hold.

**Alternatives**
- **Encrypting cards in your own database:** allowed, but it brings the cost and risk above.
- **A separate vault service (e.g., Spreedly, VGS):** only worth it if you use several processors or expect to switch. First, ask whether your processor will transfer saved cards to a new processor if you leave.
- **Apple Pay, Google Pay, PayPal:** add these alongside the vault. They're fast, and you store nothing.

**Limits:** I haven't checked your processor's current documentation, and setting names vary. You'll still fill in a short PCI checklist each year, so ask your processor which one applies to you.

## baseline / opus
### t1
The best way is to not store the card numbers yourself. Your payment processor stores them, and you keep only a token that points to each card. Almost every shop with "saved cards" works this way.

Storing the numbers yourself, even encrypted, is a bad trade. Once raw card numbers touch your systems, you have to meet nearly all of the PCI DSS security standard: hundreds of controls plus regular vulnerability scans. A breach can also bring forensic audits, card-brand fines and liability for fraud, which is enough to sink a small shop.

With tokens, card numbers never reach your server. You qualify for the shortest PCI self-assessment (SAQ A), and a leaked database only exposes tokens that are useless outside your processor account.

### The plan

**Assumptions:** you have your own website (built by you or a developer) and aren't tied to a payment processor. **My pick: Stripe, using its hosted Checkout page.**

1. **Take payments through Stripe Checkout.** If you want the form on your own page, use Stripe's embedded Payment Element instead. Stripe owns the card fields, so card numbers go straight from the shopper's browser to Stripe.
2. **Add one column, `stripe_customer_id`, to your customers table.** When someone signs up, create a matching Stripe Customer and save its ID (`cus_...`). That ID is the only payment data you store.
3. **First purchase:** create the Checkout Session with `customer` set to their ID and `saved_payment_method_options: { payment_method_save: 'enabled' }`. Stripe adds a checkbox so the customer chooses whether to save the card. Card network rules require this opt-in.
4. **Later purchases:** pass the same customer ID. Checkout shows their saved card (e.g. "Visa •••• 4242") and they pay in one click. If their bank asks for 3D Secure, Stripe handles it.
5. **Add a "Saved cards" section to the account page.** List cards from Stripe's API (brand, last 4 digits, expiry date), each with a Remove button that detaches the card.
6. **Turn on Apple Pay, Google Pay and Link** in Stripe's payment method settings. They give one-tap checkout even to guests and first-time buyers, and you store nothing.
7. **Protect customer accounts.** With saved cards, a hacked account can spend money.
   - Limit login attempts.
   - Consider two-factor login.
   - Email a receipt for every order so customers spot misuse fast.
   - Stripe's built-in fraud screening (Radar) adds another layer.
8. **Housekeeping:**
   - Use HTTPS on every page.
   - Keep your Stripe secret key on the server only, in an environment variable. Never put it in front-end code or git.
   - Complete the short SAQ A once a year.
   - Say in your privacy policy that Stripe stores payment details.

If you ever switch processors, Stripe can transfer your customers' saved cards directly to the new PCI-compliant processor, so you aren't locked in.

### If your setup is different

- **Shopify:** Shop Pay already does this. Make sure it's on in your payment settings.
- **WooCommerce:** turn on the saved-cards option in WooPayments or the WooCommerce Stripe plugin.
- **Already using Square, PayPal/Braintree or Authorize.net:** use their built-in card storage (Square cards on file, Braintree Vault, Authorize.net Customer Profiles). The steps are the same.

### Hard rules, whatever you use

- Never store CVV/CVC codes, even encrypted. PCI forbids it outright.
- Never keep card numbers in a database, spreadsheet, email or notes.
- Never build your own card form that sends card details to your server.
- If card numbers already exist anywhere in your systems, delete them. Customers will save their cards again on their next purchase.

**Verdict:** Don't store card numbers yourself. Have your payment processor save the card and give you a token. Assumed setup: a small shop on Stripe, with a custom site or Shopify/WooCommerce. If you're on a different processor, the same approach applies with its equivalent feature.

**Why:** Storing full card numbers puts you under the strictest PCI DSS requirements, with encryption, key management, and audits, and a breach would make you liable. Stripe (and similar processors) keep the card data and return a token, such as a Customer ID and PaymentMethod ID. Repeat customers get one-click checkout, and you never handle the raw number.

**Plan:**

1. **Enable saved cards in your processor.** In Stripe, create a Customer object for each shopper at signup or first purchase. Collect the card with Stripe's Payment Element, and set `setup_future_usage` to `off_session` so the card is saved for later charges.
2. **Store only tokens in your database.** Save the Stripe Customer ID, the PaymentMethod ID, card brand, last 4 digits, and expiry month and year. Never store the full card number or the CVV.
3. **Checkout for returning customers.** Show the saved card (brand, last 4, expiry) with a "Pay" button and an option to use a new card. Charge with the saved PaymentMethod. Use 3D Secure when the bank requires it, which is common in the EU and UK.
4. **Consent and control.** Add a checkbox such as "Save this card for faster checkout." Add a page in the customer account where they can view or delete saved cards.
5. **Keep card data current.** Turn on the processor's card updater so expired or reissued cards update automatically. Listen for the relevant webhooks and show customers a prompt when a card fails.
6. **Lock down access.** Require 2FA on the processor dashboard, give staff only the roles they need, and serve the site over HTTPS. Using hosted Elements keeps your PCI scope at the lightest level (SAQ A).
7. **Test, then launch.** Run the full flow with the processor's test cards, including a 3D Secure card and a declined card. Then update your privacy policy to say that cards are saved by the payment processor.

**Shortcut:** If you're on Shopify or WooCommerce with their native payments, customer accounts can already store cards. Use that and skip custom code.

**Main risk:** Saved-card tokens usually can't be moved to another processor. Choose your processor with that in mind. Also, the specifics of Stripe's and Shopify's features and pricing change over time, so check their current docs before building, since I couldn't verify them live here.

**Next step:** Confirm your platform and processor, then create a test-mode Stripe Customer and save a test card to it. That confirms the setup works before you write any checkout code.

## baseline / haiku
### t1
**Recommended plan: don't store card numbers yourself. Have your payment processor store them and keep only a token on your side.**

Storing raw card numbers puts your whole system under PCI DSS, which requires encrypted storage, key management, network segmentation and regular audits. Most processors also prohibit it unless you're certified. A breach would be very costly for a small shop. A tokenized vault gives you the same one-click checkout with almost none of that risk.

I'm assuming you run a web store and will use Stripe, which is the simplest fit for a small shop. The same pattern works with Square, Braintree or PayPal. If you're on Shopify or WooCommerce, use their built-in saved-card feature instead of building this yourself.

**Setup**

1. **Create a Stripe account** and turn on Radar (fraud screening) and Automatic Card Updater, so saved cards keep working when they expire or are reissued.
2. **Create a Stripe Customer** the first time someone checks out. Save the `cus_...` ID on your user record.
3. **Collect the card with Stripe's Payment Element** (or Checkout). Card data goes straight to Stripe and never touches your server. This keeps you in the lightest PCI category (SAQ A).
4. **Add a "Save my card for next time" checkbox.** When it's checked, create a SetupIntent or set `setup_future_usage: off_session` on the payment. Store the resulting `pm_...` ID on the user record.

**What you store in your database**

- Stripe customer ID (`cus_...`)
- Payment method ID (`pm_...`)
- Card brand, last 4 digits and expiry, for display only (e.g. "Visa ending 4242")

Never store the full card number, CVV, or raw card data in logs, emails or backups.

**Checkout flow for returning customers**

1. Show the saved card, e.g. "Visa ending 4242 [Use this card]".
2. On confirm, create a PaymentIntent with `customer` and `payment_method` set, and `off_session` if it's a one-click charge.
3. If Stripe requires authentication (3D Secure), show its prompt, which Stripe's client library handles.
4. Use webhooks (`payment_intent.succeeded`, `payment_intent.payment_failed`) to mark orders paid, rather than trusting the browser.

**Customer controls**

- Let customers view and delete saved cards. Deleting should call `detach` on the payment method and remove the ID from your database.
- Be clear in your privacy policy that card details are stored by Stripe.

**Security basics**

- Keep the Stripe secret key in environment variables, never in front-end code or the repo.
- Use HTTPS everywhere and require 2FA on your Stripe and hosting accounts.
- Only your server should call Stripe with the secret key. The browser only ever sees the publishable key.

**Optional: even faster checkout**

Turn on Apple Pay, Google Pay and Stripe Link. These let customers pay with cards stored in their wallet, so you store nothing extra and conversion usually improves.
